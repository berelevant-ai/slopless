## What Are iGaming Solutions?

Follow one registered player through a session: sign-up, a deposit, a few rounds of play, then a withdrawal request. Each step depends on software, infrastructure and operational services working in sequence. iGaming solutions is the collective name for those technology and operational solutions, the full set a company needs to launch and manage an online gambling business.

The services that carry a player through that journey include:

- Player account management (PAM) for registration, wallets and session data
- Game content and game aggregation, sourced from studios and aggregators
- Payment systems that process deposits, withdrawals and chargebacks
- Regulatory compliance tools covering identity checks and responsible gambling limits
- Customer relationship management, business intelligence and host infrastructure

An operator can buy these as one end-to-end iGaming platform or assemble them from specialists. Most modules are sold both ways: individually, for operators that already run a platform, or packaged inside a unified product. B2B services for iGaming therefore range from a single API to a complete stack, and the operator decides where to draw the line. ## B2B vs B2C iGaming Software

Picture a player who disputes a payout. The operator's support team answers the ticket, but the game round ran on a studio's servers and the withdrawal moved through a payment supplier's rails. One online casino business, two kinds of relationship: the operator serves the player, and suppliers serve the operator.

B2C iGaming software is everything the player touches on direct-to-consumer gaming sites: the lobby, the cashier, the app, the promotions. The operator earns gaming revenue from it and pays player acquisition costs to fill it. The B2B side of the gaming services sells technology to that operator instead, usually on a software-as-a-service basis. A typical contract combines a setup fee, a monthly charge and a revenue share, and the supplier's multi-tenant platform keeps each operator's data and configuration separate from every other client's.

The architecture follows the customer:

- Supplier systems are built to serve many businesses at once, with isolation, uptime and reporting as the priorities
- Operator-facing software is built around a single brand's players, with conversion and retention as the priorities
- Suppliers carry technical compliance, such as certified random number generation and audited game maths
- Operators carry licensing in each jurisdiction, plus marketing, support, VIP management and dispute handling

Studios and platform vendors supply the tools; the operator uses them to run the business and answer the player. ## Types of B2B iGaming Solutions

Getting a casino live within budget and technical limits means picking a deployment model before picking suppliers. Strategy, available capital and in-house engineering capacity point toward one of four approaches: white-label, turnkey solutions, modular integration or bespoke development. Each distributes B2B infrastructure, ownership and risk differently, and each changes what the operator's business model and iGaming stack look like a year after launch.

### White-Label Solutions

An operator with a brand, a marketing plan and almost no engineers can still start an online casino on a white-label platform. The provider owns the infrastructure, the game library, the payment connections and the compliance tooling, and the operator adds a logo, a colour scheme and a domain. Development work shrinks to configuration, which is why white-label is the fastest route to a branded launch.

The trade is control. Customisation stays within whatever the provider has built, so the digital gambling experience looks much like other sites on the same platform. Adding a payment method or a game studio outside the provider's catalogue depends on the provider's roadmap, not the operator's. Switching suppliers later means migrating player accounts and rebuilding the front end.

Licensing needs separate attention. In a strict white-label arrangement the site runs under the provider's licence, and the operator acts as a branded partner. Turnkey products differ: the operator holds its own licence and takes delivery of the full stack to run independently. The two are often sold side by side, so operators should confirm which one a contract describes. Bespoke platforms make sense when standard products cannot support the workflow a business needs: unusual agent hierarchies, specific performance targets or integrations no vendor offers. ### Modular Solutions

A casino that already runs its own front end and wants new games has a narrower problem: add one component without replacing the rest. Modular deployment solves it. Each service, from PAM to game aggregation to a sportsbook solution, connects to the existing stack through an API or an embedded iFrame. The operator keeps its interface, its player data and its cashier, and swaps or extends one module at a time.

The flexibility runs in both directions. An operator can buy only the components it lacks and replace a single service when a better one appears, which keeps the stack customisable as the business grows. Scaling happens in increments rather than through a full migration.

What moves to the operator is the integration itself. Someone in-house has to manage authentication, session handling and reconciliation between every connected service, which calls for engineers rather than configurators. One connection covers the whole studio, so the operator's integration effort stays fixed as the library expands.
