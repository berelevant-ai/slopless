# We Are Asking the Wrong Question About AI and Jobs

The dominant narrative around AI and employment goes something like this: AI will automate X% of jobs, workers will need to reskill, and the winners will be those who adapt fastest. This framing is not wrong exactly. But it misses the more important question, which is: what kind of work do we actually want humans doing?

Here is the thing that I keep coming back to after three years of working directly on AI deployment in enterprise environments: the tasks that AI handles best are, in many cases, the tasks that were also the least satisfying for the humans doing them. Data entry. Routine summarization. Template-driven document generation. Status report compilation. These are not the tasks that drew people into their professions. They are the administrative overhead that accumulated around the actual work people wanted to do.

Notably, in the organizations that are deploying AI most effectively, the reaction from employees is not fear - it is relief. The compliance analyst who spent 60% of her time extracting data from documents now spends 80% of her time on the actual risk analysis she trained for. The customer success manager who was buried in administrative handoffs is now spending that time in conversations with customers. The AI is not replacing the human. It is a force multiplier that gives the human back the work they found meaningful.

Importantly, this is not a universal experience. There are tasks and even entire job categories where AI displacement is real and the workers affected do not have an easy path to higher-value work within their organization. Those situations deserve serious attention and serious policy responses. The risk of the "AI will empower everyone" narrative is that it gets used to avoid confronting the real disruption that is happening in specific sectors.

But the dominant framing - AI as a threat to be managed - paves the way for organizational responses that are defensive rather than transformative. It is worth noting that the companies producing the strongest results are the ones that asked "what becomes possible for our people when AI handles the routine?" rather than "how do we minimize AI disruption?"

The question is not whether AI will change work. It will. The question is what we choose to build with that change.
