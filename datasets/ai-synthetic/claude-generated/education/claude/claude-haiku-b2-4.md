# Why Digital Literacy Is Now a Civil Rights Issue

I want to talk about something that doesn't get framed strongly enough: digital literacy isn't just about being able to use a computer anymore. It's about access to opportunity, to information, to economic participation, and increasingly, to civic engagement itself.

We're living through a moment where digital skills are table stakes. If you can't navigate online platforms, understand basic data concepts, or manage your digital identity, you're effectively locked out of major institutions - banking, healthcare, education, employment. The question of who has these skills has become a question of equity.

Here's what concerns me: the digital divide has evolved. It's no longer just about who has internet access, though that remains a real problem in many regions. It's increasingly about depth of capability. Someone might have a smartphone, but lack the literacy to protect themselves from scams, to evaluate information critically, or to leverage digital tools for economic advancement.

This creates a compounding advantage for those who grew up with technology and compounding disadvantage for those who didn't. And it breaks along familiar lines - by income, by geography, by age, by educational background.

The implications are substantial. A generation of older adults faces barriers to digital participation that limit their economic independence and social engagement. Young people from under-resourced communities may have devices but lack quality training in how to use them effectively. Immigrant populations may face language and cultural barriers to digital proficiency.

What's really at stake is opportunity. Digital literacy increasingly determines access to job markets, educational resources, financial services, and civic participation. If we're serious about equity and inclusion, we have to be serious about digital literacy.

This isn't just a government problem or a nonprofit problem. Businesses have a role here too. Companies benefit from a digitally literate customer base and workforce. There's a business case for investing in digital literacy initiatives - both as corporate social responsibility and as long-term risk management.

So what needs to happen? We need comprehensive digital literacy infrastructure. This means training programs that go beyond basic computer skills - programs that cover digital citizenship, privacy, critical thinking about information, and practical skills for economic participation. We need these programs accessible and affordable across communities. We need to support teachers and librarians as digital literacy educators. And we need buy-in from the private sector and government both.

The good news is there's real momentum building. Nonprofits, libraries, schools, and forward-thinking companies are all working on this challenge. But we need to scale these efforts dramatically.

This is a long-term investment in human potential and societal health. And I'd argue it's one of the most important educational imperatives we face.
