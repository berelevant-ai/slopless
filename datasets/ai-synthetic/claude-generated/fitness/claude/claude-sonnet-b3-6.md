# 7 Things You Should Know About Cardio Training

Cardiovascular exercise is a cornerstone of overall health and fitness, but there is often a lot of confusion about how to approach it effectively. Should you do steady-state cardio or high-intensity intervals? How much is enough? How much is too much? Here are seven key things that will help you make smarter decisions about your cardio training.

## 1. Not All Cardio Is the Same

It is important to understand that there are fundamentally different types of cardio training, and each one produces somewhat different adaptations in your body. Low-intensity steady-state cardio, like a brisk walk or easy jog, builds aerobic base and is easy to recover from. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) is more demanding but can improve cardiovascular fitness and burn more calories in a shorter period of time. Both have a place in a well-rounded program.

## 2. HIIT Is Effective but Easy to Overdo

High-intensity interval training has received a tremendous amount of attention in recent years, and for good reason - it is genuinely time-efficient and effective. However, because of its high demand on your body, it should not be done every day. Two to three HIIT sessions per week is typically plenty for most people. More than that significantly increases the risk of overtraining, burnout, and injury.

## 3. Steady-State Cardio Has Unique Benefits

Despite the rise of HIIT, there is real value in longer, lower-intensity cardio sessions. Activities like cycling, swimming, walking, or jogging at a comfortable pace are excellent for building your aerobic base, improving heart health, and supporting active recovery. They are also much easier to sustain as a long-term habit because they do not leave you completely exhausted.

## 4. Cardio Does Not Have to Mean the Treadmill

One of the most powerful things you can do for your cardio fitness is to find activities you actually enjoy. Running on a treadmill is just one option among many. Swimming, cycling, rowing, hiking, dancing, playing recreational sports, and even brisk walking all count as valuable cardiovascular exercise. Choosing activities that you genuinely look forward to is the most sustainable long-term strategy.

## 5. Zone 2 Training Is Having a Well-Deserved Moment

If you have been paying attention to the fitness world recently, you may have heard a lot about Zone 2 training. This refers to low-intensity cardio performed at roughly 60 to 70 percent of your maximum heart rate, where you can still hold a conversation. Research increasingly supports Zone 2 training as one of the best things you can do for long-term cardiovascular health and metabolic efficiency.

## 6. Cardio and Strength Training Work Together

A common concern is whether doing cardio will interfere with building muscle or strength. In moderate amounts, cardio and strength training are highly complementary. Cardio improves your cardiovascular capacity, which actually helps you perform better in strength training sessions and recover faster between sets. The key is to not do excessive amounts of cardio that leave you too fatigued to train effectively with weights.

## 7. Consistency Over Intensity Is the Long Game

Just as with other forms of exercise, consistency is the most important variable in cardio training. Three to five moderate cardio sessions per week, sustained over months and years, will deliver far greater health and fitness benefits than occasional intense bursts of exercise separated by long periods of inactivity. Building cardio into your regular routine in a way that is sustainable is always the smartest approach.

Understanding how cardio training actually works gives you the tools to build a smarter, more effective fitness routine and to keep your cardiovascular health strong for years to come.
