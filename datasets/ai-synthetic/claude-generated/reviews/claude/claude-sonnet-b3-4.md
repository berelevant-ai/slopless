# 7 Things You Should Know About Employee Performance Reviews

Performance reviews are one of the most consequential processes in any workplace. They shape careers, influence compensation, and affect the relationship between managers and their teams. Whether you are giving or receiving a performance review, understanding how they work can help you get significantly more value out of the process.

## 1. Preparation Is What Separates Good Reviews From Great Ones

Many managers and employees walk into performance review meetings with little preparation and end up having vague, unproductive conversations. The most effective reviews happen when both parties have done their homework. Managers should review notes, project outcomes, and documented feedback from the entire review period. Employees should prepare a self-assessment and come ready to discuss their achievements and challenges.

## 2. Recency Bias Is a Real and Significant Problem

Human memory is not well-suited to evaluating performance across a full year. We naturally tend to weight recent events more heavily than things that happened many months ago. This is known as recency bias, and it can lead to wildly inaccurate assessments. The solution is to document performance continuously throughout the year rather than relying on memory when review time arrives.

## 3. Vague Feedback Is Almost Useless

Telling someone they are "a great team player" or that they "need to improve their communication" gives them very little to work with. Effective feedback is specific, behavioral, and tied to observable outcomes. What exactly did the person do well, and in what situation? What specific behaviors need to change, and what would a better approach look like? Concrete feedback is the only kind that actually helps people grow.

## 4. Two-Way Dialogue Produces Better Outcomes Than One-Way Delivery

Performance reviews should never feel like a verdict being handed down from on high. The most productive review conversations are genuine dialogues where employees have ample space to share their perspective, raise concerns, and discuss their goals. Managers who listen well during reviews learn things that make them better leaders and help the entire team perform more effectively.

## 5. Goals Set During Reviews Need to Be Realistic and Measurable

It is very common for performance reviews to end with a set of goals for the coming period, and it is equally common for those goals to be forgotten within weeks. Goals that are vague, unmeasurable, or completely unrealistic are unlikely to be achieved. The most effective goals are specific, have a clear timeline, and can be objectively evaluated at the next review cycle.

## 6. Ratings and Rankings Often Create More Problems Than They Solve

Many organizations use numerical ratings or forced rankings to evaluate employee performance, but research consistently shows that these systems can damage morale, discourage collaboration, and introduce bias. If your organization uses ratings, be aware of their limitations and make sure the qualitative conversation is the primary vehicle for meaningful feedback.

## 7. The Annual Review Should Never Be a Surprise

If an employee is genuinely surprised by the content of their annual performance review, something has gone wrong throughout the year. Managers who provide regular, ongoing feedback ensure that formal reviews simply document and reinforce conversations that have already happened. This makes the process far less stressful and far more useful for everyone involved.

Rethinking how you approach performance reviews, whether as a manager or an employee, can genuinely transform the professional development experience in your organization.
