## 000039-008

In a paired comparison, each participant tries both versions of a task. This reduces variation caused by stable differences between people, because each person supplies their own comparison. It can make a small experiment more informative than assigning a different group to each version, especially when baseline skill varies widely across the class.

Order still matters. A participant who sees the same puzzle twice may solve it faster on the second attempt, regardless of which interface is easier. Researchers can counterbalance the order by giving half the participants version A first and the other half version B first. The puzzle items also need comparable difficulty. Otherwise, an easier second set could be mistaken for an improvement caused by the interface.

The paired design gives rise to a reduction in the influence of stable individual differences. Since the same participant supplies a result for each version, differences in that person's baseline ability are held steady in the comparison. That is why a paired experiment can extract more information from a small class than a design with separate groups.

For Thursday's exercise, record completion time and errors on separate columns. A faster response with more errors may represent a trade-off rather than an improvement. Keep the participant codes consistent across the two versions so that the results can be paired during analysis.

## 000039-028

A correlation can remain almost perfectly linear even when the measurements have a small systematic error. Suppose every thermometer in a study reads half a degree too high. The temperature ranking of the rooms may still be correct, and the relationship with heating demand may still form a straight line. The offset changes the reported temperatures without necessarily changing how closely the points follow that line.

Random error has a different effect. If each reading drifts by an unpredictable amount, the points scatter around the underlying relationship. Taking repeated measurements can help estimate that variation, provided the errors are not shared across all readings. Repeating the same faulty instrument reading many times will not reveal its fixed offset. A reference thermometer is needed to test the calibration.

When writing the lab report, separate the question of association from the question of accuracy. Report the correlation for the observed relationship, then state how the thermometers were checked against the reference. A tight line alone cannot justify a claim that the temperatures are accurate. Include the calibration date and the largest difference found, so a reader can judge the size of the uncertainty alongside the strength of the association.

## 000039-029

To clean a fountain pen, separate the barrel from the grip and remove the cartridge. Hold the grip over a bowl and flush cool water through it with a bulb syringe made for pen cleaning. Stop when the water runs clear. Water clears loose ink, while dried deposits may need a longer soak in a second bowl of clean water.

Keep the cap out of the soaking bowl if it has a metal liner. Wipe its inside with a damp cloth instead, and leave it open to dry. A cotton bud can catch on the inner clip mechanism, so use a twisted corner of soft cloth where space is narrow. Check the manufacturer's instructions before removing a nib or feed; some are fitted tightly enough that pulling them can damage the grip.

At reassembly, hold the grip over a white tissue and flush a little more water through it. Clear water on the tissue confirms that soaking has loosened the deposits that survived the first rinse. Dry the outside, then leave the parts apart overnight so water inside the feed can evaporate. Fit a fresh cartridge the next day and write a few lines on scrap paper. The first line may look pale while the remaining moisture mixes with the ink.
