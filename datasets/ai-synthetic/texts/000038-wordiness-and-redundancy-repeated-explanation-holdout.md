## 000038-129

Why a paint sample looks different at home

A paint colour is seen in the light that falls on it. The shop's bright overhead lighting may be quite different from the daylight and lamps in your room. A small card held beside other colours can also look different from a large painted wall.

Buy a sample in the finish you intend to use if one is available. Paint two coats onto a large piece of plain card and let it dry fully. Move the card around the room rather than testing only beside the window. This makes it possible to compare the same painted surface in places with different light.

Look at the sample at the times when you normally use the room. A bedroom seen mainly under warm bedside lamps may need a different decision from a kitchen used in morning daylight. Hold the card vertically on a wall rather than flat on a table, since the light reaching it will change with its angle.

Nearby materials matter too. A large green curtain can reflect colour onto a pale wall. Put the sample beside the flooring and furniture you will keep, not just beside a new cushion you may later move elsewhere. If the room is empty during renovation, bring back a representative piece of fabric before deciding.

The useful final test is therefore at home, on a reasonably large sample, in your usual light and beside the materials that will remain. This combines the lighting and surrounding-colour checks into one decision. Record the product, finish and mixing code when you buy, so a later touch-up can begin with the same specification even if the shop changes its display.

## 000038-157

Names as historical data

A surname is an identifier used by people and institutions, not a permanent label attached to one unique person. Two residents may share a name, and one resident may appear under several spellings. A family-history search therefore needs supporting attributes such as address, occupation, age, or household relationships.

These attributes also change. A person can move, change work, or report an approximate age. The aim is to assemble a plausible sequence of records rather than require every field to remain identical. A contradiction matters in proportion to its significance: an age difference of one year may be routine, while two simultaneous residences far apart require explanation.

Administrative practices add another complication. A clerk may expand initials in one ledger and abbreviate them in another. Married names, patronymics, and naming conventions can be handled differently across institutions. Inspecting neighbouring entries often reveals a convention that is difficult to recognize from an isolated database result.

When records are linked at scale, the non-unique nature of names creates a further consequence. A common surname can generate many possible matches, so an algorithm that always chooses the closest-looking name may create false connections. Leaving a record unlinked can preserve the distinction between an uncertain match and an established identity. A dataset should make those unresolved cases visible rather than quietly treating them as absent people.

Researchers can document a sample of accepted and rejected links, including difficult common-name cases, to test the linking rule. The report should identify which attributes were used, how conflicts were handled, and whether a human checked ambiguous results. That information allows later users to assess the kinds of family or migration patterns the linked records can support.

## 000038-174

Building a household appliance folder

Use the model number from the appliance's own rating plate when saving its manual. A product's shop name can cover several versions with different controls and parts. The number on the machine is the link to the instructions that actually apply to it. Photograph the plate if it is awkward to read.

Create one folder per appliance, using an ordinary name that everyone in the household recognizes. Put the manual, receipt, and installation details inside. Keep the purchase date in a separate note if the receipt is likely to fade, and record the retailer's order reference while it is still easy to find.

For service visits, write down the date, the reported symptom, and what was replaced or adjusted. A short description such as 'stopped draining during the rinse' is more useful than 'broken again.' Include a photograph of any displayed error code before clearing it. These details can prevent a later technician having to reconstruct the history from memory.

When downloading instructions, rely on the number printed on the appliance rather than its retail name, since the same name may describe versions with different parts and controls. Store the downloaded file locally so it remains available if the manufacturer's page changes.

Receipts can become unreadable over time, so copy the purchase date into the folder note instead of relying on the original paper to preserve it. Add warranty correspondence and any confirmation that a repair was completed. If the appliance is sold or given away, pass on the manual and relevant service history while removing payment information and unrelated personal details.

Once a year, delete folders only for appliances that have left the household, after checking whether you still need their purchase records. Keep the remaining folder names consistent so someone else can locate them when you are away.

## 000038-164

Cleaning glass from a picture-frame work area

If a frame's glass breaks, keep children and animals out of the room and put on sturdy shoes before approaching it. Small fragments can travel beyond the visible pile. Work from the clear edge toward the break so you do not step through the area you are trying to clean.

Use stiff cardboard to lift the larger pieces into a rigid container. Avoid carrying them loose in a thin rubbish bag, where sharp edges can puncture the side. Check the frame backing before moving it: pieces may be trapped between the backing and the remaining glass. Wrap or contain those parts before taking them across the room.

Once the large pieces are removed, use a light held low to the floor to look for smaller fragments. Follow the cleaning method appropriate to the floor surface and inspect nearby furniture feet, skirting edges, and the underside of any mat. A patterned rug can conceal fragments that are easy to see on a plain floor.

When the work area looks clear, keep your shoes on while removing the protective sheet and emptying the cleaning materials. Folding the sheet can expose glass that was hidden underneath it, and fragments may have lodged in a brush. Treat this final handling as part of the cleanup rather than the point when the room is already safe to use.

Seal the rigid waste container and label it as broken glass before disposal under the local collection rules. Inspect the route used to carry it out, then check the soles of your shoes. Let others back into the room only after the floor and nearby surfaces have received a final inspection under good light.

## 000038-113

Understanding a phone's storage warning

A storage warning concerns the space used by files and apps on the phone. It is different from a low-memory message about what the phone can handle while programs are running. Buying cloud storage may help move some files off the device, but it does not automatically remove their local copies.

Start with the storage page in settings. It should show which categories take the most space. Video often occupies more than a large number of text messages, so deleting messages at random can be a frustrating way to free a few gigabytes. Check downloaded films, offline maps and podcasts that you have finished.

Read the deletion message before confirming, and test your understanding with an unimportant picture if the wording is unclear.

The distinction about cloud space becomes important when you choose a photo setting. An option labelled optimise may retain small previews on the phone while storing full-sized originals online. An option that merely uploads a copy can leave the original taking up space locally. Those two settings can produce the same online library but different amounts of free phone storage.

After removing a large download, revisit the storage page rather than judging by whether the warning disappears instantly. Some apps keep recently deleted files for a recovery period. Emptying that area can free space sooner, but it also removes the easy way back. Keep enough local room for updates as well as the files you plan to add.

## 000038-155

Studying an old map

An old map's scale should be checked before distances are inferred from it. Printed copies can shrink unevenly, and a later reproduction may have been resized to fit a page. Measure a known grid interval on the particular copy being used rather than assuming that its stated scale remains accurate.

Symbols require a different kind of checking. A line that resembles a modern road may represent a parish boundary, a footpath, or a proposed route. The key is the first reference point, followed by comparison with other maps from the same publisher. Handwritten additions should be distinguished from the printed layer because they may record a later survey.

The map's blank areas also need interpretation. Woodland drawn as a decorative cluster of trees may indicate a broad land category without locating individual trees. Buildings may be enlarged to remain visible at the chosen scale. These conventions make the map useful for navigation or administration while limiting the precision of measurements taken from individual symbols.

### Describing the copy

A catalogue reference should identify the sheet, edition, and repository. If the analysis uses a digital image, record whether it was downloaded from a scan of the original or a photograph of a later facsimile. Folds and binding edges can obscure small features, and software may straighten a curved page in ways that alter local geometry.

Before deriving distances, verify the scale against a known grid interval on the actual copy, since resizing and uneven shrinkage can make the printed scale unreliable. Image resolution should be recorded separately: more pixels may reveal a faint label, but they cannot recover information that was absent from the source. Where a boundary remains uncertain, the analysis should preserve that uncertainty rather than tracing a falsely precise line.

## 000038-152

The meaning of a cool surface

A wet cloth can become cooler than the surrounding air as water evaporates from it. Evaporation draws energy from the cloth and its immediate surroundings, lowering the cloth's temperature. The amount of cooling depends partly on how readily water vapour can move away from the surface.

This makes airflow relevant. A gentle current of air carries humid air away and replaces it with air that can accept more vapour. In still conditions, a moist layer develops close to the cloth. Humidity also affects the process: when the surrounding air is already close to saturation, evaporation proceeds less readily and the difference between wet and dry measurements becomes smaller.

A classroom comparison can use two similar thermometers, one with a damp covering around its sensing end. The instruments should first be checked together under the same dry conditions. If their readings differ substantially before the covering is added, that difference needs to be understood before interpreting the later comparison. Students should also allow time for readings to settle.

The damp covering cools because water leaving its surface as vapour takes energy from the covering and nearby material. This energy transfer explains why the wet thermometer can read below the surrounding air temperature. Keeping the covering wet during the observation is important, since a drying patch changes the surface being measured.

The result is best recorded with the room conditions and the time allowed for equilibration. Students can then compare observations from different days without assuming that a fixed temperature gap is a property of the cloth itself. The experiment demonstrates an interaction among a surface, the surrounding air, and the movement of moisture.

## 000038-147

Why a percentage needs its denominator

A library report says that requests for large-print books rose from ten percent to twenty percent of all reservations. That change does not by itself show that the number of large-print requests doubled. The share can rise because other reservations fell while large-print requests stayed constant. Percentages describe a relationship between a category and its total, so changes in either quantity affect the result.

Suppose the first month contained twenty large-print requests among two hundred reservations, while the second contained twenty among one hundred. The percentage doubled, but the desk handled the same number of large-print requests. For staffing, the count may be the more relevant measure. For deciding how to divide shelf space among formats, the share may still be useful.

Time periods also need to match. A four-week reporting period and a five-week reporting period expose the service to different numbers of opening days. Dividing by opening days can help compare daily workloads, provided that closures and shortened hours are recorded. A monthly total alone can conceal a busy week followed by a closure.

Charts introduce further choices. A line showing percentages is easier to interpret if the accompanying table includes counts and totals. When the total is small, one additional request can move the percentage considerably. Showing those underlying values allows readers to judge the scale of the change.

The central implication is that a higher percentage of large-print reservations need not mean more such reservations: a fall in the other requests can enlarge the share while the large-print count stays unchanged. Reports should also preserve the category definitions, especially if audiobooks or interlibrary requests have moved between categories during the year.

## 000038-145

Reading absence in an archive

A missing name in a register can have several explanations. The person may never have been recorded, the relevant page may have been lost, or the name may appear under a spelling that a modern search does not recognize. Before treating silence as evidence of absence, a historian needs to understand how the register was produced and preserved.

Consider a town's apprenticeship records. If registration was required only for trades governed by a guild, the books describe a regulated part of working life. Domestic service and informal family training may appear elsewhere or remain undocumented. The selection occurred when the records were created, before any later damage or cataloguing decisions.

Survival introduces a different filter. Water damage might destroy a continuous run of volumes, while a fire might leave scattered fragments from many years. A gap that affects every trade in the same period suggests a preservation problem. A gap confined to one trade calls for closer attention to the rules and practices of registration. Comparing the physical volumes with the catalogue can help locate that distinction.

Search tools create a third layer. A transcription may render a difficult surname incorrectly, and a database may index only the master rather than the apprentice. Trying spelling variants is useful, but so is checking which fields the search covers. A search returning no result reports the behavior of the index, not an inspection of every surviving page.

Taken together, creation, survival, and retrieval define what an apparent absence can support. The practical sequence is to check who was eligible for recording, identify gaps in the surviving series, and inspect the index's coverage. Only then can the missing name become evidence for a historical claim rather than merely a description of an unsuccessful search.

## 000038-149

Stocks, flows, and a crowded shelter

A shelter's occupancy is a stock: the number of people present at a particular time. Admissions and departures are flows measured over an interval. Put simply, occupancy is a count at a moment, while admissions count movement into the shelter during a period. Confusing them can make a routine report difficult to interpret.

Suppose a shelter begins Monday with forty residents, admits twelve people, and records nine departures before midnight. It ends the day with forty-three residents. The twelve admissions matter for intake work, even though occupancy rises by only three. Staff may need to process identification, allocate bedding, and explain rules for each arrival. A small change in occupancy can coexist with a busy intake desk.

Length of stay links the two measures over longer periods. If residents remain longer, beds become available less frequently, even with unchanged admissions. A service can then become full while turning away applicants whose needs never appear in its admission totals. Records of refused or deferred requests provide information that occupancy alone cannot supply.

The distinction also changes how a chart should be drawn. Occupancy may be recorded at midnight each day, whereas admissions may be totaled for the full calendar day. Connecting the daily occupancy points is reasonable, but the admissions series should retain its interval meaning. A reader should be able to see whether a plotted value refers to one moment or a span of time.

For planning a second intake desk, managers would examine arrivals by hour and the work required for each arrival. For planning additional beds, they would examine occupancy peaks and lengths of stay. The two decisions use related records, but they require different measures of pressure on the service.

Getting a bike ready after winter

## 000038-160

Comparing household budgets

Household spending figures should be adjusted for household size before being used to compare living standards. A budget supporting five people cannot be interpreted in the same way as the same budget supporting one. Dividing expenditure among members is a simple starting point, although it does not capture all the benefits of sharing a home.

## 000038-154

Why a transcription has gaps

A transcript represents choices about what features of speech to preserve. A version prepared for studying turn-taking may record pauses and overlap, while a version prepared for a public oral-history website may omit many hesitations. Removing a pause changes the evidence available about timing even when every spoken word remains.

The recording offers a useful reference, but access to it may be restricted by consent agreements. Researchers should therefore describe their transcription conventions and explain what an empty bracket, a dash, or an uncertain word means. Consistency allows readers to distinguish a speaker's interruption from an editor's notation.

Background sounds can matter as well. Laughter from other people may explain a speaker's response, while a passing vehicle may account for an inaudible phrase. A transcript made from a close microphone can preserve a voice clearly while leaving the wider interaction obscure. Notes about the recording setup help establish which sounds were available to the transcriber.

Pauses deserve particular attention in this context. Editing out silences alters the timing evidence, even if the transcript retains all of the words that were spoken. The analyst can also distinguish an audible hesitation from a delay caused by searching for a photograph or reading a document. Those actions may be visible only in field notes or video.

A published extract should identify any departure from the full transcript's conventions. Combining several turns into a fluent paragraph may be useful for presentation, but the change should be recorded where conversational structure is relevant.

The main point is that retaining the words while removing pauses still changes what can be inferred about the timing of the exchange. A citation should lead readers to the particular transcript version used in the analysis.

## 000038-144

A river through three scales

At a bend in a river, the strongest current is often displaced toward the outer bank. Faster flow can erode that bank while slower water deposits sediment along the inner bank. Over successive floods, these uneven changes can shift the channel sideways. A diagram showing a single bend captures this local movement reasonably well.

The same bend looks different when the unit of study is an entire floodplain. Material removed from one bank may be deposited a short distance downstream, so local erosion does not necessarily represent sediment leaving the river system. Floodwater can also spread fine material across fields beyond the channel. A sediment budget needs to specify the boundaries within which gains and losses are counted.

Time introduces another distinction. A field team surveying after one storm might observe rapid bank retreat, yet the long-term position of the channel can remain within a relatively narrow corridor. Vegetation establishes on new deposits, abandoned bends fill with sediment, and later floods may redirect the main flow. The rate inferred from a single event should therefore be tied to the interval it describes.

Return now to the outer bank. For a landowner whose fence is approaching the water, movement of several metres is consequential even if the floodplain's total sediment storage remains stable. For a reservoir manager downstream, the relevant quantity may instead be the sediment transported beyond the whole reach. These are compatible descriptions because they ask about different boundaries.

Choosing a scale is part of defining a research question. A useful account names the length of river, the period observed, and the material being tracked before presenting a rate. Otherwise two accurate measurements can appear to disagree simply because their units of observation differ.

## 000038-122

Choosing a dehumidifier for a spare room

The litres-per-day figure on a dehumidifier is measured under stated test conditions. It is not a promise that the machine will collect that amount in your spare room every day. Cooler air and a lower starting humidity can produce much less water, even when the machine is working normally.

Start by deciding what the room needs. A spare room used for drying laundry has a recurring source of moisture. A room that became damp after a single leak may need a temporary drying period and a check that the leak has stopped. Buying a larger machine will not repair water entering through a damaged roof.

How you will empty it

A machine with a small tank may stop before a load of laundry has dried. Check whether it can drain through a hose and where that hose must run. Some arrangements rely on gravity, so a bucket on a high shelf is not a workable destination. Keep access to the tank clear if you plan to empty it by hand.

Tank capacity and extraction rate describe different things. The tank tells you how much water can be held before emptying; the daily figure describes extraction under the maker's test conditions. Actual collection in a cool, fairly dry room can be much lower than that advertised rate without indicating a fault.

Where it will stand

Read the clearance requirements before choosing a position behind a chair. The unit needs room to draw air in and release it again. Check the noise level if the spare room doubles as an office, and consider whether the display can be dimmed when guests stay. Castors are useful only if the route to the sink has no steps that require lifting.

Compare machines using tests made at similar temperatures and humidity levels. A high headline number measured in warm, humid air is difficult to compare with a lower number measured in cooler air. Look for the conditions beside the figure rather than comparing the largest numbers on the boxes.

Once the machine is running, the amount in the tank should be interpreted with the room conditions in mind. A low daily collection can be normal in a cooler room with less moisture to remove, since the rated daily output is a test result rather than a guaranteed yield in that room. Follow the manual's cleaning schedule and inspect the filter before a dusty renovation job nearby.

## 000038-156

Estimating a queue from photographs

A photograph of a queue records the people present at one instant. It does not show everyone who waited during the preceding hour. Someone who arrived, was served quickly, and left between photographs will be absent from the images. A sequence of snapshots therefore measures occupancy more directly than total demand.

The spacing of photographs determines which changes can be observed. Images taken every half hour may miss a brief surge after a train arrives. Taking images more often improves time resolution but creates more counting work. The sampling interval should be chosen in relation to the service's expected rhythm rather than the convenience of the camera timer.

Counting rules also matter. People may stand beside the line while waiting for a friend, or one person may hold a place for a group. A study needs an observable rule, such as counting people within a marked waiting area who are facing the service point. The rule will have limitations, but applying it consistently makes the measurement interpretable.

Finally, waiting time cannot be read directly from queue length without information about arrivals and service. A long line moving quickly can produce a shorter wait than a small line stalled by a complicated transaction. Timing a sample of individual visits can supply the missing dimension.

For a fieldworker preparing the camera schedule, these points become four checks:

1. Define the marked area and the counting rule.
2. Choose an interval short enough to capture expected surges.
3. Describe the image counts as people present, rather than total visitors.
4. Add timed observations if the question concerns waiting duration.

Keeping those decisions with the images allows another observer to repeat the count and understand what the resulting series measures.

## 000038-116

Why a small heater can still use plenty of electricity

A heater's physical size does not tell you its electricity use. A compact heater rated at two kilowatts draws roughly the same power at full output as a larger heater with the same rating. The case may be smaller while the heating element still converts electricity at the same rate.

To estimate use, start with the power setting and the time the element is actually on. A thermostat can interrupt heating once the room reaches its set temperature. A timer limits the hours of operation instead. The two controls answer different questions, and many heaters have both.

Placement affects how well the heat serves you. A heater tucked behind furniture may warm an inconvenient corner while the rest of the room remains uncomfortable. Follow the maker's clearance instructions and keep fabrics away from the unit. Check whether an extension lead is permitted before using one.

Choosing a small casing is not an energy-saving decision by itself. At equal power ratings, the compact heater and the large one can consume electricity at the same rate when their elements are on.

For a room used briefly, compare how quickly a heater supplies warmth where you sit, not just how warm its outer surface becomes. For a room used all evening, thermostat behaviour may matter more. Keep doors closed where appropriate so you are heating the intended space.

The controls do separate jobs: a thermostat switches heat according to room temperature, whereas a timer determines when the heater may run. Understanding that difference helps explain why a heater can be powered but not producing heat. Read the indicator description in the manual before treating that pause as a fault.

## 000038-151

Remembering a laboratory procedure

A researcher can remember how to prepare a sample without being able to state every movement involved. Repeated practice makes some actions familiar: how much pressure to use on a cap, when a liquid has mixed sufficiently, or which sound indicates that a small pump has started. This practical memory supports reliable work but can be difficult to transfer through written instructions alone.

Training therefore often combines a written procedure with observation. A new colleague watches an experienced worker, attempts the task, and receives corrections. The corrections make previously unspoken judgments visible. Asking the experienced worker to describe what prompted an intervention can reveal a missing condition in the procedure, such as a cloudy sample that requires additional preparation.

An institution also 'remembers' a procedure, but in a different sense. Its memory consists of documents, retained samples, equipment settings, training arrangements, and people whose experience overlaps. When one employee leaves, these resources can preserve continuity. A procedure stored on a shared drive contributes little if nobody knows which version is current or how to locate the necessary materials.

The two forms of memory can come apart. A laboratory may retain an expert who performs a task well while losing the records needed to explain why a particular setting was adopted. Conversely, it may preserve a detailed protocol after everyone familiar with its practical difficulties has departed. Both situations create a specific vulnerability when unusual samples arrive.

To study continuity, researchers should therefore ask different questions at each level. At the individual level, observation can reveal learned judgment. At the institutional level, tracing a procedure through records and staff changes can show how that judgment is preserved, distributed, or lost.

## 000038-141

Why a station average can move

A town's annual temperature average can change even when none of its thermometers records a different local climate. Suppose a monitoring network loses two hilltop stations and keeps its valley stations. The remaining measurements will be warmer because the network now samples lower ground. A shift in the places being measured can therefore produce a shift in the average without a corresponding change at any individual site.

This matters when comparing a modern series with records assembled from paper registers. Earlier observers often worked at schools or railway offices, whereas later instruments may have been installed at airports. The move can alter exposure to buildings, shade, and paved surfaces. A station history helps an analyst distinguish these changes from a regional temperature signal. Missing entries also matter: an average based mostly on summer months should not be compared directly with a complete calendar year.

One approach is to compare each station with nearby stations over the years when their records overlap. A change confined to one instrument becomes easier to identify against that background. Such comparisons require care in mountainous areas, where nearby sites may have substantially different seasonal patterns. The analysis should preserve the original observations alongside any adjusted series so that another researcher can reproduce the decisions.

Network composition remains an additional consideration during this work. If cool, elevated sites drop out and warmer valley sites remain, the overall mean rises simply because different locations contribute to it, even though conditions at the continuing stations have not changed. Reporting how many stations contributed each year makes the coverage visible. A map of station openings and closures can be more informative than a single total.

What a waiting-list comparison measures

Imagine that a university offers a voluntary writing workshop and compares participants' final essays with those of students who did not attend. The participants may already have been more motivated, had lighter schedules, or received encouragement from a tutor. A difference between the two groups would combine the workshop's influence with differences that existed before anyone entered the room.

A useful report therefore states both the assignment procedure and the population from which participants were recruited. Those details tell a reader which decision the estimate can reasonably inform.

## 000038-143

Borrowing and sound change

Loanwords provide evidence about contact between speech communities, but their modern pronunciation does not simply preserve the sound of the source language. Speakers fit unfamiliar sounds into the categories available in their own language. A borrowed word may therefore reveal the receiving language's sound system as much as the donor's pronunciation.

Dating the borrowing is a separate problem. A word first recorded in a surviving manuscript may have circulated in speech for decades. The earliest written occurrence establishes that the word was in use by that date; it does not establish the moment when speakers first adopted it. Scholars combine written evidence with trade records, settlement histories, and the distribution of related forms.

### Comparing forms

A promising comparison examines several words believed to have entered during the same period. If they undergo similar substitutions, the pattern may support a shared route of transmission. A single unusual form could instead reflect a dialect, a copying error, or an intermediate language. Modern speakers also sometimes reshape an unfamiliar borrowed form to resemble a familiar native word.

Consequently, the pronunciation of a loanword reflects adjustment to the sound categories of the language receiving it, rather than serving as a direct recording of the donor language.

### Using documents

Manuscripts differ in spelling conventions, and an editor's normalized transcription may conceal variation present on the page. Consulting images can resolve whether two printed forms represent distinct historical spellings or a modern editorial choice. An index of names is often useful because names preserve forms excluded from a general dictionary.

Finally, the date of the first surviving written example tells us the word existed by then, not when borrowing began. A responsible account gives a date range where the evidence permits one and identifies the documents on which that range depends.

Coding disagreement in interview research

When two researchers assign different codes to the same interview passage, the disagreement can reveal an unclear category boundary. They should return to the passage, explain what each code captures, and revise the coding guidance if the distinction cannot be applied consistently. Simply choosing the more senior researcher's code would conceal the ambiguity instead of resolving it.

A useful comparison begins with a manageable sample of transcripts. Researchers code that sample independently, then examine both agreements and disagreements. High agreement on obvious passages can coexist with a recurring problem in a small but important category. A single overall percentage may hide that concentration, so disagreements should also be grouped by code.

The coding scheme may change during this stage. If a broad category is divided, earlier transcripts need to be revisited under the new guidance. Keeping a versioned codebook makes that work traceable. Where coders diverge, the appropriate response is to discuss the original passage and clarify the category definitions rather than allow seniority to determine the answer. Some projects also distinguish descriptive codes from interpretive ones. Agreement on whether a participant mentions a waiting period may be easier to achieve than agreement on whether the passage expresses distrust. Those different tasks should be evaluated with their purposes in view.
