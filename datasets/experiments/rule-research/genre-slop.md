# Genre-Specific AI/SEO Slop Patterns

Research compiled 2026-06-01. Feeds rule design for slopless. Each entry has a signal note and, where relevant, a false-positive (FP) note.

---

## 1. Recipe Blogs

### Pre-Recipe Padding Openers
- "Without further ado" — classic pivot phrase signaling a long intro finally ending
- "Let's get started" — hollow transition before instructions
- "I remember the first time I made this" — fabricated personal origin story
- "My grandmother used to make this" — synthetic nostalgia, AI has no grandmother
- "This recipe holds a special place in my heart" — manufactured sentiment
- "The story behind this recipe" — signals a padded origin section
- "I've been making this for years" — false experiential authority
- "This dish brings back so many memories" — unfounded emotional anchor
- "It all started when" — intro narrative that delays the recipe

### Recipe-Versatility Padding
- "This recipe is perfect for" (followed by a list of 3+ occasions) — generic occasion stacking
- "Whether you're [X] or [Y], this recipe is for you" — false inclusivity scaffold
- "Great for weeknight dinners, holidays, or meal prep" — triple-occasion filler
- "You can make this for the whole family" — generic appeal assertion

### Quality/Approval Padding
- "You'll love this recipe" — unearned prediction
- "Trust me, you won't be disappointed" — false promise with no basis
- "This is hands-down the best [dish] you'll ever make" — superlative assertion without test
- "Even picky eaters will love it" — audience-widening filler
- "The results speak for themselves" — avoids specifics

### "Good News" Softeners
- "The good news is" — softening opener with no informational value
- "The best part is" — teaser padding before an obvious point
- "Here's the thing about" — throat-clearing before a basic fact
- "What makes this recipe special is" — self-congratulatory setup

### Structural Tells
- Recipe introduction exceeding ~300 words with no ingredient or technique content — pure SEO padding
- "Jump to Recipe" button present while intro runs 400+ words — signals intentional delay
- Repeated keyword insertions ("easy chicken recipe", "simple chicken dinner") mid-prose — keyword stuffing masquerading as prose

### False-Positive Notes
- Personal anecdotes ARE legitimate in recipe blogs written by actual humans; the signal is synthetic-sounding anecdotes with no concrete detail (no specific year, no named person, no place)
- "Perfect for weeknights" is fine once; the FP risk is low when it appears in isolation

## 2. Medical / Health Content

### Disclaimer Boilerplate (Legal-Cover Hedges)
- "This is not medical advice" — standard but signals undifferentiated AI output when it appears verbatim
- "Consult your doctor before making any changes" — safe-harbor phrase, near-universal in AI health content
- "Always consult a qualified healthcare professional" — same pattern, more formal variant
- "Speak with your healthcare provider" — softer variant of the same hedge
- "This content is for informational purposes only" — appears in virtually every AI health article
- "Individual results may vary" — supplement/wellness boilerplate
- "This article does not constitute medical advice" — legalistic variant

### Vague Attribution Hedges
- "Studies show that" (no citation) — assertion of evidence without a source
- "Research suggests" (no citation) — same
- "Experts agree" (no citation) — false consensus claim
- "According to health professionals" — unanchored vague authority
- "Many people find that" — low-evidence generalization
- "It is widely believed that" — passive-authority construction

### Softening/Over-Qualification Clusters
- "Generally speaking" — pre-emptive caveat weakening the claim
- "In many cases" — vague frequency hedge
- "It's important to understand that" — meta-commentary before a basic point
- "It's important to note that" — same, more formal; appears ~31x more often in AI text than human text
- "It should be noted that" — bureaucratic variant
- "Keep in mind that" — reader-management filler

### Generic Health Calls to Action
- "Take control of your health today" — motivational filler with zero specificity
- "Start your wellness journey" — "journey" slop applied to health
- "Small changes can make a big difference" — empty platitude
- "Listen to your body" — vague directive masquerading as advice

### False-Positive Notes
- Medical disclaimers ARE legally required on health websites; the signal is when they appear as the only hedging mechanism with no actual evidence cited anywhere in the article
- "Studies show" is legitimate when a citation follows; the FP risk is low if a DOI or publication name is present

## 3. Legal Content Explainers

### Disclaimer Boilerplate
- "This is not legal advice" — near-universal in AI legal content; signal when it's the only nuance offered
- "Nothing in this article creates an attorney-client relationship" — legalese boilerplate
- "You should consult an attorney" — safe-harbor redirect
- "Laws vary by state" — geographic hedge, sometimes true but used as blanket cover
- "Laws vary by jurisdiction" — same, international variant
- "This information may not apply to your specific situation" — hedge that eliminates article utility

### Complexity-Navigation Phrases
- "Navigate the complexities of" — applies to nearly any legal topic; pure filler
- "The legal landscape" — metaphor substituting for specifics
- "The ever-changing regulatory environment" — temporal filler
- "Understanding your rights" — topic label masquerading as content
- "It can be confusing, but" — patronizing setup before a basic explanation

### Over-Hedged Explanations
- "Generally speaking" — pre-emptive disclaimer
- "In most cases" — vague frequency that avoids committing
- "Depending on your circumstances" — generic out-clause
- "This is a complex area of law" — statement that forestalls explanation
- "It's important to note that" — meta-commentary before a basic point
- "One might argue that" — AI's false-perspective hedging

### Structural Tells
- Every paragraph has 3-4 sentences (uniform paragraph length in legal explainers signals AI)
- Headers on every paragraph — over-formatted AI output
- Excessive bullet points converting explanations into lists without legal nuance
- No case citations, statute references, or jurisdiction-specific details — absence of real legal knowledge

### False-Positive Notes
- "Laws vary by state" can be genuinely true and necessary — signal is when it appears without specifying which states or what the variation is

- "Believe in yourself" — platitude with no actionable content

## 5. Product Reviews

### Fake-Balance Structure
- "Pros and cons" section that lists 3 pros and 1 minor con — structural fake-balance
- "No product is perfect, but..." followed by a trivial flaw — token criticism
- Cons section containing only: "slightly expensive", "learning curve", "packaging could be better" — generic cons that apply to any product
- Every "con" is framed as a non-issue: "While the price is higher, the quality justifies it"

### Conclusion Scaffolding
- "In conclusion" — mechanical closing signal; 42 phrases AI can't resist #39
- "All in all" — variant
- "Bottom line" — reviewer's summary formula
- "Final verdict" — pseudo-authoritative closer
- "Final thoughts" — softer variant
- "Overall" (as a paragraph opener for the conclusion) — structural tell

### Quality / Value Assertions
- "Worth every penny" — value affirmation with no comparative basis
- "Game changer" / "game-changer" — overused significance amplifier; flagged by multiple AI detection sources
- "Highly recommend" — unearned endorsement formula
- "I was blown away" — faked emotional reaction
- "Exceeded my expectations" — generic satisfaction formula
- "Completely transformed how I [do X]" — transformation formula applied to products
- "I can't imagine life without it" — hyperbolic dependency claim

### Fake-Testing Language
- "After using this for a few weeks" — vague duration (AI cannot test products)
- "In my experience" — false authority claim
- "I've tested dozens of similar products" — unverifiable expertise claim
- "The first thing that struck me" — scripted impression opener

### Structural Tells
- Exact same "pros and cons" structure across multiple reviews (template detection)
- Star rating contradicts written sentiment — rating/text mismatch
- Vague praise that applies to any product: "great quality", "very useful", "does what it says"
- No measurements, durations, model numbers, or version specifics

### False-Positive Notes
- "In conclusion" and "overall" appear in human writing; FP risk is moderate — weight them only in clusters with other signals

## 7. Listicles

### Opening Scaffolding
- "Let's dive in" — canonical listicle opener after the intro
- "Without further ado" — pivot phrase before list begins
- "Let's get started" — variant
- "In this article, we will cover" — meta-commentary opener
- "In this article, we'll explore" — variant
- "In this guide, you'll learn" — guide-format variant
- "Here are [N] [things] that" — mechanical list introduction
- "We've compiled a list of" — compiler-voice filler

### Intra-List Scaffolding
- "Next up" — item-to-item transition filler
- "First on our list" / "Number one on our list" — redundant ordinal narration
- "Rounding out our list" — closing-item transition
- "Last but not least" — stock closing phrase
- "Honorable mention" — pseudo-section for overflow items
- "That's not all" — artificial teaser for more items
- "And the list goes on" — trailing acknowledgment of incompleteness

### Closing Scaffolding
- "There you have it" — closing summary transition
- "We hope this list has been helpful" — approval-seeking close
- "Which of these resonated with you?" — engagement-bait close
- "Let us know in the comments" — generic CTA close

### List-Item Padding Patterns
- Each item restates the headline of the list ("This is one of the best tools because it is a great tool") — circular definition
- "It's as simple as that" after each explanation — minimizing closer
- Identical sentence structure across all N items — template uniformity signal

### Structural Tells
- Every section has exactly 2-3 sentences (metronomic uniform length)
- Numbers in title don't match actual items in article
- "Top 10" with items ranked by no discernible criteria

### False-Positive Notes
- "Let's dive in" appears occasionally in human writing; signal is when it follows a 3+ paragraph intro padding section in a listicle format

- "Leverage" is legitimate in financial writing; weight only in contexts where it is used as a generic verb for "use"
- "Comprehensive" appears in legitimate course or service descriptions; signal is when used in article titles/headings as a content-quality claim with no evidence

## Cross-Cutting Structural Signals (All Genres)

### Opening Patterns
- Article opens with a definition: "X is a [noun] that [basic definition]" — dictionary-opener slop
- Article opens with a statistic without a citation source
- Article opens with "Have you ever wondered..." — question-hook slop
- Article opens with "Imagine..." or "Picture this..." — scene-setting slop opener

### Closing Patterns
- Final paragraph restates the opening paragraph — bookend redundancy
- Closes with "We hope this [article/guide/post] has been helpful" — approval-seeking close
- Closes with a rhetorical question: "Which tip will you try first?" — engagement-bait close

### Transition Patterns
- "Now that we've covered X, let's move on to Y" — section-navigation narration filler
- "But that's not all..." — artificial tease between sections
- "Here's where it gets interesting" — mid-article manufactured drama

### Attribution Patterns
- "Research shows" with no citation
- "Studies indicate" with no citation
- "According to experts" with no named expert
- "Many [professionals/doctors/lawyers/experts] believe" — vague authority consensus
