Three years ago, I told my team we didn't need AI tools. "We hire smart people," I said. "Let's not outsource our thinking."

I was wrong. And understanding exactly how I was wrong changed the way I lead.

The mistake wasn't being skeptical of technology. Skepticism is healthy. The mistake was confusing the tool with what the tool replaces. I thought AI would replace judgment. It doesn't. It replaces the slow, costly, error-prone work between judgment and outcome -- the first drafts, the repeat checks, the late-night reviews that turn out weak because humans get tired and tools don't.

When I finally sat down with the team and started using these systems alongside them, something shifted. The question stopped being "will this replace us?" and started being "what does this free us to do that we couldn't before?"

That reframe is the leadership lesson.

Every big technology shift in my career -- cloud, mobile, open-source software -- followed the same arc. Early adopters seemed reckless. Late adopters seemed slow. The leaders who built the most lasting value asked a different question at the start: not "should we use this?" but "what does this make possible that was once too costly or too slow?"

AI is no different, except the adoption cycle is compressed. The window between "early" and "late" has shrunk to months.

What I've seen watching strong teams use these tools: the bottleneck was never talent. It was time. Skilled people were spending 40% of their time on tasks that needed care but not their unique skills. AI gave that time back. And when skilled people get their time back, they do what they were hired to do -- think harder, push further, and catch things no tool will ever catch.

The leaders I've seen struggle with AI share one pattern: they treat it as a trade -- do we replace people or not? That frame makes every choice feel like a threat. Shift the frame and you see it clearly -- you're not picking between your team and a tool. You're picking how much of your team's time goes to the work only they can do.

Learning the tools is easy -- an afternoon does it. Harder is updating your mental model of what a strong team looks like when their capacity grows.

I got that update late. The teams I work with now got it early. The difference in output is not subtle.

If you're still waiting to see how this plays out, you're already behind the teams you're competing with. The question now is how quickly you can close the gap.
