# Is it advisable for a healthy male in his early 40s to take the influenza vaccine?

Influenza vaccines do not have satisfactory effective rates of preventing flu. Flu vaccination reduces the risk of flu illness by between 40% and 60%
Vaccines aren't meant to stop diseases by making 100% invulnerable individuals. Of course we want the highest possible efficacy, but the primary benefit is the large impact they have on a collective level, by limiting the spread of a disease, so that it snuffs itself off.
It's all about reducing the  basic reproduction number ( R  0 ) , the number of cases of the disease that are caused by infection from one case of the desease. There are 3 ranges this number can fall into
1 <  R  0  Each person, on average, infects more than 1 person before recovering. Disease spreads as more people get infected than people are recovering.
R  0  = 1 Each person, on average, infects exactly 1 other person before recovering. The disease spread plateaus (i.e. the number of infected remains the same.), as the number of people getting infected exactly matches the number of people recovering.
0 ≤  R  0  < 1 Each person, on average, infects less than 1 person before recovering. The disease gets snuffed out over time, as people recover faster than people are getting infected.
The exponential math that models disease spread applies in many areas, such as in nuclear chain reactions, where the  effective neutron multiplication factor  is the average number of neutrons from one fission that cause another fission. Just as  control rods  are used in nuclear reactors to maintain the  k  around 1 (a stable, non-growning, non-dying rate of reaction), vaccines can be used to bring  R  0  below 1, so that deceases snuff themselves out.
