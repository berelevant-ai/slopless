# The Most Valuable QA Skill in the Age of AI Is Thinking

A few years ago, AI in testing was a futuristic conversation. Now it's a daily one, the share of US employees using AI at work has [more than doubled in two years](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/699689/ai-use-at-work-rises.aspx), from about a fifth to nearly half. Every week brings a new model, a new coding assistant, another "AI-powered" testing platform, and one more confident prediction that engineers and testers are about to vanish.

So the question keeps coming: is AI going to replace testers?

The honest truth…partially, yes. Not because testing stops mattering, and not because human judgment suddenly goes obsolete. It's because a large share of testing work has always been deterministic, and deterministic work is exactly where AI thrives. The real shift was never whether AI replaces QA. It's whether testers learn to work with it.

---

## The real divide isn't AI vs. testers

The framing is usually wrong. It's not AI versus humans, automation versus manual, or machines versus engineers.

The divide that actually matters is between testers who adapt and testers who don't, and the labor data already shows it happening. A [Harvard Business School working paper](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/enhance-or-eliminate-how-ai-will-likely-change-these-jobs) that analyzed nearly all US job postings from 2019 through early 2025 found that after ChatGPT launched, demand for jobs heavy in structured, repetitive tasks fell about 13%, while demand for roles built on analytical, technical, and creative work grew about 20%. The author's read is blunt: AI isn't simply eliminating jobs, it's shifting demand toward work where human-AI collaboration is the point.

That's the divide in a nutshell. The engineers using AI well are moving faster: generating ideas, debugging quicker, exploring edge cases sooner, automating the repetitive stuff, and simply producing more. Everyone else is still stuck treating AI as either a gimmick or an existential threat. It's neither. It's a co-pilot, and that distinction turns out to matter a lot.

---

## Augmented intelligence, not artificial intelligence

A lot of AI conversations collapse two very different things into one word. There's AI that augments humans, and AI that replaces them, and most of what people actually do with it today is the first kind. [Anthropic's Economic Index](https://www.anthropic.com/economic-index), which analyzes millions of real Claude conversations, found usage leaning toward augmentation, roughly 57% augmentation to 43% automation in its first report, and still augmentation-led (about 52% to 45%) a year later.

The split even tracks the tool: the conversational app skews collaborative, while the autonomous coding agent, Claude Code, ran closer to 79% automation. That's the whole distinction in one dataset. Augmented intelligence makes a person more capable while leaving them in control, Tesla's Autopilot, AI-assisted diagnostics, a debugging assistant. The system helps; the human still owns the call.

QA engineers are unusually well-positioned to get this, because testing has always run on validation, skepticism, and risk. We already think in terms of confidence, verification, edge cases, and failure conditions. In the age of AI, those instincts become more valuable, not less.

---

## Deterministic work vs. indeterministic thinking

This is the mental model that reframed the whole thing for me.

AI is excellent at deterministic tasks, work with clear patterns, repeatable structure, and defined success criteria. And it's getting better at them fast: a [METR study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499) found that the length of task an AI agent can complete at 50% reliability has been doubling roughly every seven months for six years. The detail that matters most for us is where that reliability lives. Those same models score near-100% on tasks that take a human under about four minutes, but under 10% on tasks that take a human more than about four hours. That cliff is the deterministic/indeterministic line drawn in real data…AI crushes the short, well-scoped work and falls apart as tasks stretch long and ambiguous.

You can see the migration happening in testing-adjacent work, too. In Anthropic's usage data, the share of interactions spent creating new code more than doubled (4.1% to 8.6%) while time spent debugging and fixing fell, a sign the routine deterministic work is steadily moving onto the machine.

Where AI still struggles is the indeterministic stuff, decisions soaked in ambiguity, business context, tradeoffs, and risk tolerance.

> Should we ship this feature?  
> Is this level of risk acceptable?  
> Does this workflow actually make sense for a real user?  
> Are we even testing the right thing?

None of those are pattern-matching problems.

They take judgment, and judgment is still stubbornly human.

## How I'm actually using AI today

These days, Claude Code is woven into every phase of our Software Development Lifecycle and the shape of how we use it is the whole point. It isn't "AI writes the tests now." It's a loop where the machine moves fast and a human stays on the controls at every handoff.

In practice it breaks into four phases, and depending on a ticket's size and difficulty we might reach for AI in one of them or all of them.

**Planning.** I can point Claude at a Linear ticket directly through a Model Context Protocol (MCP) integration, MCP being the open standard that lets Claude read from tools like our issue tracker instead of me copy-pasting context in. From there it reads the ticket, reviews our existing Cypress suite, and documents where we should update coverage or add new tests. Instead of starting from a blank page, I start from a draft I can argue with.

**Writing.** Next I can have it write those tests, leaning on Claude Skills for our Cypress conventions. Skills are basically packaged know-how, a folder of best practices and examples Claude loads on demand, so the tests come out matching how we structure things, rather than generic boilerplate it guessed at.

**Debugging.** When something flakes or fails, I point Claude at the evidence: a CI run in GitHub, or a screenshot and stack trace locally. It's genuinely fast at the pattern-matching part of triage, faster than I am at 4pm on a Friday… three cups of coffee deep and crashing.

**Review.** I'll also have it review the change in a separate session, a fresh context that never saw the earlier conversation, so it's a real second set of eyes instead of an echo of the first.

The thread running through all of it is human validation. Every phase has a manual checkpoint: I'm reading the plan, pushing back on the test design, sanity-checking the fix. We use a planning plugin called Superpowers to keep that structure visible, but the tool matters far less than the principle, the checks, the input, and the final call stay with a person.

The value was never that AI replaced my role. It's that it amplified the parts I was already good at, and let me move faster on exactly the tickets where I chose to.

## Confidence is not correctness

Here's the part the industry still underplays.

We've had bad code forever.

The new risk isn't that AI is occasionally wrong, it's that people stop interrogating the output because the machine sounds so sure of itself.

A [Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon study](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/) of 319 knowledge workers found that the more people trusted the AI, the less critical thinking they applied, while the more they trusted their own expertise, the more they scrutinized what came back. The same study found that AI doesn't remove critical thinking so much as relocate it: toward verifying information, integrating responses, and stewarding the overall task. Which is to say the thinking doesn't go away. It moves to exactly the places testers already live.

AI is remarkably good at producing confident answers: clean code, polished summaries, authoritative recommendations. But confidence and correctness are different things, and in testing that gap is everything. The job has never been about producing outputs, it's about questioning them. The irony of this era is that the easier answers are to generate, the more valuable it becomes to challenge them.

That's where complacency creeps in. Over time it gets easy to accept generated code without checking it, trust an explanation too quickly, skip the deeper investigation, to quietly outsource the reasoning itself. And the better AI gets, the more dangerous that passivity becomes, because wrong answers stop looking obviously wrong.

Consider the failure modes that never trip an alarm. An AI tells you every unit test passes and coverage is 100%. Reassuring until you notice nobody tested the new regulatory requirement, the workflow technically functions but fails accessibility, or the UI automation is green while the actual experience is miserable. Those aren't deterministic failures. They're judgment failures. Which is exactly why thinking is worth more now, not less.

---

## The most valuable skill is no longer execution

For years, testing interviews leaned on tool knowledge, framework experience, and how fast you could implement automation. That still counts. But as AI absorbs more of the repetitive implementation work, the differentiator shifts toward something harder to fake: reasoning, decomposition, validation, investigation, communication, skepticism.

In a word, thinking.

The testers who thrive over the next decade won't be the ones who type scripts fastest. They'll be the ones who ask better questions, spot better risks, validate output instead of trusting it, understand the system deeply enough to know when the AI is wrong, and pair their own judgment with the machine's speed. That combination is hard to beat.

---

## Testing AI is becoming its own discipline

One more shift worth watching: increasingly we're not just using AI, we're testing it. And AI systems break the assumption traditional automation is built on, that input A reliably produces output B. Outputs vary. Confidence fluctuates. Models drift. Edge cases turn probabilistic instead of binary.

That pushes QA toward a different toolkit: repeated-run validation, confidence thresholds, drift monitoring, adversarial testing, golden datasets, uncertainty management. And this is no longer fringe, there's now formal scaffolding for it.

Consider the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework). NIST, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, is the federal agency that writes the technical standards whole industries quietly run on, and when it publishes a framework, that framework tends to become the shared baseline everyone else converges on. So it's telling that NIST has now turned the same machinery on AI. The framework, released in 2023 and later extended with a dedicated generative-AI profile, organizes the problem around four functions: govern, map, measure, and manage.

And look at what those four functions actually are: not buttons to press, but judgment calls.

- **Governing** means deciding how much risk is acceptable.
- **Mapping** means understanding the context a system runs in.
- **Measuring** means choosing what's even worth measuring, and reading the results honestly.
- **Managing** means weighing the tradeoffs and deciding what to act on.

The official, government-backed blueprint for testing AI turns out to be a blueprint for structured human judgment. That's the quiet confirmation of everything above: when even the standards body writes down how to test AI, what it writes down is a discipline built on thinking, not execution.

--- 

## Final thoughts

A lot of people are asking how long until AI replaces them.

The more useful question is how we evolve alongside it.

The future of QA probably isn't fully human or fully autonomous, it's collaborative. The testers who succeed won't be the ones avoiding AI, and they won't be the ones surrendering their judgment to it either.

They'll be the ones who use it without handing over the thinking. Because in an industry about to be flooded with generated answers, the ability to question them might be the most valuable QA skill there is.

With that in mind, happy thinking and as always, happy testing.
