# 7 Things You Should Know About Employment Contracts

Starting a new job is an exciting milestone, but before you sign on the dotted line, it is essential to understand what you are agreeing to. Employment contracts can have long-lasting implications for your career and your rights as a worker. Here are seven things every employee should know before signing an employment agreement.

## 1. Not All Employment Is "At-Will"

In many US states, employment is "at-will" by default, meaning either party can end the relationship at any time for almost any reason. However, an employment contract can change this significantly. A contract may specify the duration of employment, grounds for termination, and required notice periods. Understanding whether your employment is at-will or contract-based is a fundamental distinction.

## 2. Non-Compete Clauses Are Increasingly Scrutinized

Non-compete agreements restrict your ability to work for competitors after leaving a job. While they are still common, courts in many states are increasingly skeptical of overly broad non-competes, and some states like California largely refuse to enforce them at all. The Federal Trade Commission has also moved to limit their use at the national level. Review any non-compete carefully and understand your state's rules.

## 3. Intellectual Property Assignments Can Be Broad

Many employment contracts include clauses that assign ownership of anything you create during your employment to the employer. Some of these clauses are remarkably broad and may even cover work you do on your own time if it is related to the company's business. It is incredibly important to read these provisions carefully and, if necessary, carve out personal projects before you sign.

## 4. Arbitration Clauses Limit Your Legal Options

A growing number of employment contracts require disputes to be resolved through private arbitration rather than the court system. This can significantly limit your rights, particularly in cases involving discrimination or wage theft. Arbitration proceedings are typically confidential and often favor employers statistically. Knowing this trade-off before you sign is essential.

## 5. Severance Is Not Automatic

Many employees assume they are entitled to severance pay if they are laid off. In reality, severance is only required if your contract or company policy specifically provides for it. If you want severance protections, they need to be negotiated and included in your agreement before you start. This is especially relevant for senior-level positions.

## 6. Confidentiality Agreements Survive Employment

Non-disclosure agreements - or NDAs - typically remain in force long after your employment ends. This means you may be restricted from discussing certain business information for years, or even indefinitely. Understanding what you are agreeing to keep confidential, and for how long, is a critical part of reviewing any employment contract.

## 7. Everything Is Negotiable Before You Sign

Many job seekers feel awkward negotiating contract terms, but it is completely normal and expected - especially for professional roles. Things like title, compensation, equity, work location, and even certain restrictive covenants can often be modified. Once you sign, your negotiating power drops significantly. Taking the time to negotiate upfront is always worth it.

Employment contracts are serious legal documents that deserve careful attention. When in doubt, having an employment attorney review your contract before signing can be one of the best investments you make in your career.
