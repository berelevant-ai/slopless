# 7 Things You Should Know About Credit Scores

Your credit score is one of the most important numbers in your financial life, yet a surprising number of people do not fully understand how it works or why it matters so much. A strong credit score can open doors to better loan rates, lower insurance premiums, and even improved job prospects. Here are seven essential things everyone should know about credit scores.

**1. Your credit score is calculated using five key factors**

Most credit scores, including the widely used FICO score, are based on five main factors: payment history, amounts owed, length of credit history, new credit, and credit mix. Payment history carries the most weight at 35 percent, followed by amounts owed at 30 percent. Understanding these factors is the first step toward actively improving your score.

**2. There is not just one credit score**

This is something that surprises a lot of people. You actually have multiple credit scores, not just one. There are different scoring models, such as FICO and VantageScore, and each of the three major credit bureaus - Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion - may have slightly different information on file for you. As a result, your score can vary depending on which bureau and which model is being used.

**3. Payment history is by far the most important factor**

If there is one thing you take away from this article, let it be this: paying your bills on time is the single most impactful thing you can do for your credit score. Even one missed payment can have a significant negative impact that takes months or even years to fully recover from. Setting up automatic payments is a simple and effective way to ensure you never miss a due date.

**4. Your credit utilization ratio matters enormously**

Credit utilization refers to the percentage of your available credit that you are currently using. For example, if you have a credit card with a $10,000 limit and you are carrying a $3,000 balance, your utilization rate is 30 percent. Most financial experts recommend keeping your utilization below 30 percent, and ideally below 10 percent, for the best possible impact on your score.

**5. Checking your own score does not hurt it**

There is a persistent myth that checking your own credit score will lower it. This is simply not true. When you check your own score, it is recorded as a "soft inquiry," which has no impact on your credit score whatsoever. Hard inquiries, which occur when a lender pulls your credit as part of an application, can have a small temporary impact. You should feel free to monitor your score regularly.

**6. Negative information does not stay on your report forever**

Most negative items, including late payments, collections, and charge-offs, will fall off your credit report after seven years. Bankruptcies can remain on your report for up to ten years. While this timeline might sound discouraging, the good news is that the impact of negative items diminishes significantly over time, especially as you build positive credit history on top of them.

**7. Building credit takes time, but there are smart shortcuts**

If you are starting from scratch or trying to rebuild damaged credit, patience is genuinely required. However, there are some strategies that can accelerate the process. Becoming an authorized user on a family member's credit card account, opening a secured credit card, or taking out a credit-builder loan are all proven approaches that can help you establish or rebuild a positive credit history more quickly.

Understanding your credit score and taking active steps to improve it is one of the most financially rewarding things you can do for your long-term financial health. The effort you put in today will pay dividends for years to come.
