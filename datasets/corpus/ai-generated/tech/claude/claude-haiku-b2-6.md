# Open Source as Infrastructure: Why the Licensing Model Matters More Than Ever

I want to discuss something that I think is often overlooked in tech strategy conversations, and that's the role of open source in enterprise infrastructure. Specifically, I'm talking about how open source licenses have become a critical business decision, not just a technical one.

There was a time when open source was something you used cautiously, if at all. Enterprise IT wanted vendor support, predictable updates, and someone to sue if things went wrong. Open source projects run by volunteers didn't fit that picture. They still don't, not entirely, but the equation has shifted dramatically.

Today, open source is absolutely foundational to modern infrastructure. The vast majority of software running on the internet - your web servers, your databases, your container orchestration platforms - is built on open source. At this point, trying to build something non-trivial without open source is economically irrational for most organizations.

But here's where it gets interesting: the licensing model for open source has become a strategic flashpoint. And I think that tells us something important about where the market is heading.

For years, permissive licenses like MIT and Apache 2.0 dominated. These licenses basically said: do whatever you want with this code, commercial or not, with minimal restrictions. They enabled huge ecosystems because there was no licensing friction. But they also meant that cloud providers could take open source software, add minimal value, and sell it as a service without contributing back.

This has led to a licensing arms race of sorts. We're seeing more projects adopt licenses like the Elastic License or the Server Side Public License - licenses designed to allow open source use but prevent cloud providers from simply wrapping the code and selling it as a service without negotiation.

Why does this matter? Because it reflects a fundamental tension in the open source ecosystem:

Open source developers want recognition and sustainability. But if your project becomes so successful that cloud providers can commercialize it without any relationship with you, where's your incentive to continue investing?

Cloud providers want to offer compelling services, and open source components are often the core of that value. But they also have incentives to not contribute back or help sustain the projects they depend on.

Companies building products want access to good open source. But if the licensing becomes restrictive or unpredictable, they might invest in alternatives or build proprietary solutions instead.

What's happening now is a maturation of the open source business model. We're seeing projects adopt licenses that are sustainable for maintainers while still being permissive enough for most users. We're seeing cloud providers negotiate commercial arrangements with projects. We're seeing venture capital flow into open source companies that can create sustainable models.

This is healthy, actually. For open source to remain viable as infrastructure, it needs to be economically sustainable for the people building it.

If you're making tech decisions for your organization, the licensing conversation should be front and center. It's not just legal due diligence; it's a strategic decision about which projects you depend on and how aligned your incentives are with their long-term viability.

The future of infrastructure is open source. But that future only works if the open source ecosystem itself is sustainable.
