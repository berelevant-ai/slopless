# Rethinking Higher Education: The Skills Gap and the ROI Question

I've been having some interesting conversations lately with students, parents, and employers about a question that feels increasingly urgent: Is a four-year degree still the right path?

This isn't meant to be controversial. It's meant to be honest. The traditional higher education model is under pressure from multiple angles. The cost of tuition has climbed at rates that far outpace wage growth. Graduate unemployment or underemployment remains a sticky problem. And employers increasingly talk about skill gaps - even among degree holders - suggesting that traditional education may not be keeping pace with what the job market actually needs.

Here's what I think is happening: We're in a transitional moment. For decades, a degree served as a reliable signaling mechanism - a credential that said "this person has baseline knowledge and can finish what they start." That signal still matters, but it's no longer sufficient on its own.

The winners in this environment aren't necessarily those with the most degrees. They're those with relevant, demonstrable skills paired with continuous learning mindsets. This is changing how we should think about education - both during and after formal schooling.

We're seeing really interesting alternatives emerge. Coding bootcamps, online certification programs, apprenticeships, and competency-based credentials are all gaining traction because they're more efficient. They're focused, shorter, and directly tied to employer needs. And for many career paths, they deliver faster returns on investment than traditional degrees.

But - and this is important - the answer isn't to abandon higher education. For many fields, for graduate-level knowledge, and for the kind of deep thinking that takes time to develop, universities remain invaluable. The shift is more nuanced than "college or not college."

What's really changing is what education looks like across a lifetime. Instead of frontloading learning into your twenties, we're moving toward continuous upskilling. Instead of a single credential, you might accumulate a portfolio of skills, certifications, and experiences. Instead of asking "What degree should I get?" young people might ask "What's my learning strategy for the next five years?"

This requires institutions - both traditional and new - to adapt. It means thinking differently about curriculum, about how credentials are structured, about partnerships between schools and employers.

The good news? There's genuine momentum behind innovation in education. The conversation is changing. And that creates real opportunity for those willing to lead.
