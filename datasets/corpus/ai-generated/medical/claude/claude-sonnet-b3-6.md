# 7 Things You Should Know About Antibiotics

Antibiotics have transformed modern medicine, saving countless lives since their discovery. But they are also one of the most misunderstood and misused categories of medication. Knowing how they work - and how to use them responsibly - is genuinely important for both your own health and for public health more broadly. Here are seven key things to understand.

## 1. Antibiotics Only Work Against Bacteria

This is perhaps the most important thing to get clear: antibiotics are effective against bacterial infections, not viral ones. Colds, the flu, most sore throats, and COVID-19 are all caused by viruses, which means antibiotics are completely ineffective against them. Taking antibiotics for a viral illness does nothing to help you recover faster and carries real downsides.

## 2. Antibiotic Resistance Is a Serious Global Problem

Every time antibiotics are used - especially unnecessarily - there is an opportunity for bacteria to develop resistance to them. Antibiotic-resistant infections are already responsible for a significant and growing number of deaths worldwide, and some bacteria have become resistant to nearly all available treatments. This is one of the most pressing public health challenges of our time, and individual prescribing choices contribute to it at scale.

## 3. You Should Always Finish the Full Course

A very common mistake people make is stopping their antibiotic course early once they start feeling better. The problem is that the bacteria causing the infection may not be fully eliminated yet. Stopping early can allow surviving bacteria - which may be the hardier, more resistant ones - to rebound and cause a more difficult-to-treat reinfection. Completing the prescribed course as directed is important.

## 4. Antibiotics Affect Your Gut Microbiome

Antibiotics do not selectively target only the bacteria causing your infection - they affect the broader community of bacteria living in your gut as well. This can cause temporary digestive upset, and in some cases it creates an opening for problematic organisms like Clostridioides difficile to proliferate. Probiotic-rich foods or supplements are commonly recommended during and after antibiotic use to support microbiome recovery.

## 5. Not All Antibiotics Are the Same

There are many different classes of antibiotics, and they work through different mechanisms and target different types of bacteria. A broad-spectrum antibiotic that worked for a past infection may not be the right choice for a different one. Prescribers ideally choose the most targeted antibiotic appropriate for the specific infection, which is another reason self-medicating with leftover antibiotics is a bad idea.

## 6. Some Infections Resolve Without Antibiotics

Not every bacterial infection requires antibiotic treatment. Many ear infections, sinus infections, and mild urinary tract infections can resolve on their own with supportive care. In these cases, a "watchful waiting" approach is sometimes recommended, particularly in otherwise healthy adults. Overprescribing antibiotics for conditions that would resolve anyway is a meaningful contributor to resistance.

## 7. Allergic Reactions Can Be Serious

Antibiotic allergies - particularly to penicillin-type drugs - are among the most commonly reported drug allergies. Reactions can range from mild rashes to severe anaphylaxis. It is important to inform every healthcare provider about any known antibiotic allergies, and to understand that many people who believe they are allergic to penicillin may not actually be - formal allergy testing can clarify this and open up treatment options.

Antibiotics remain one of medicine's most powerful tools, but their effectiveness depends on using them wisely. Understanding when they are and are not appropriate is one of the most practically useful things a patient can know.
