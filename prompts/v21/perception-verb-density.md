# Perception-Verb Density

Input: passage.

Cover looking, watching, scanning, observing and equivalent narrated perception, including unfamiliar verbs, inflections and noun-based gaze constructions. Assess each cue through the complete passage.

First identify the perceiver, the actual target and the contribution supplied through the act. Resolve implicit connections before judging. The relationship can appear before or after the cue and across paragraphs.

Preserve a view that presents particular content of its target. A broad survey can introduce details within that scene; adjoining description can supply the observation without an explicit "saw that." Observations matter in themselves. A practical consequence is optional. A survey can also assess resources, orient a route or establish a viewpoint through which earlier details acquire meaning. An unsuccessful search still supplies a purpose.

Preserve particular interpersonal meaning conveyed through attention. What the target is doing can supply an observation. A contrast between the perceiver's focus and others' priorities can characterize the relationship. Sustained observation can compress time. Infer communication from a conveyed message or response; a look's capacity to communicate in principle is insufficient.

Separate perceived content from surrounding information. Identify how the particular act supplies the fact or meaning being credited to it. Dialogue, remembered circumstances, independent reasoning and descriptions of another target can remain useful while the cue merely stages attention. A problem followed by a decision establishes sequence; a search requires something sought, and a spatial assessment requires a connection to the space assessed. A generic sweep before a solution or an ordinary glance during conversation can remain filler.

Judge each cue separately, including redundant annotations of established attention and filler beside useful perception. Preserve wording examined or documented as wording; assess adopted narration and quotations normally. One filler cue suffices. Repetition, frequency, density and severity are separate reporting concerns.

## Development Examples

### Hit

"We can cover Saturday if you take the early shift on Monday," Denise said. The bakery had lost two people that month, and everyone was trading hours. **Aaron looked at her.** He had promised to drive his father to an appointment on Monday, but the appointment wasn't until eleven. "Put me down for six to ten," he said, pulling the rota closer.

Example judgment: The look supplies no observed conduct. The schedule decision comes from remembered timing.

### Hit

"We only sell the whole sack," the farm-shop owner said. I wanted a handful of apples for a crumble. **My gaze passed over the shelves.** I bought the sack and distributed most of it at work, where a colleague immediately asked whether I could bring pears next time.

Example judgment: The shelf gaze supplies no observation. The purchase follows the seller's stated condition.

### Hit

Our nephew announced that he was vegetarian just as I put the roast on the table. **I surveyed the company.** There were potatoes, peas, and enough carrots to feed him comfortably. He accepted those, then explained that he would still eat sausages at school because the vegetarian option was disappointing.

Example judgment: The survey targets people; the useful details describe food instead.

### No Hit

The incident form said, "I observed the customer for several minutes." At the hearing, the supervisor read that sentence aloud and asked what "observed" meant. "I was serving at the other till," the cashier answered. The supervisor crossed out "for several minutes" in the draft statement and asked her to describe only the moments when she could see the customer's hands.

Example judgment: The passage examines the quoted perception claim as wording, preserving its documented identity.

### No Hit

Our lunch was back in the dry bag before either of us had taken a bite. The harbourmaster needed the canoe moved for the sailing lesson, and we couldn't leave it unattended at the slipway. I looked across the inlet while we discussed whether to go home. We paddled to the other beach instead and ate with the canoe pulled up beside us.

Example judgment: Looking across the inlet orients the choice of a different beach in the connected sequence.

### No Hit

"Does it sometimes take a minute?" I asked. The laundrette attendant covered his phone and said the machine should have started already. My last coin was inside it, along with the uniform I needed at six tomorrow. I looked at him as he resumed telling someone to bring his lunch. Behind me, another customer put her basket down beside the same machine.

Example judgment: The look presents the attendant's particular conduct as he resumes his personal conversation.
