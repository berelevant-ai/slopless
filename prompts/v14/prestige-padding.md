# Prestige Padding

Does the writer or narrator use reputation, credentials, experience, famous associations, media attention, or audience size as support for a claim beyond what that status establishes? Find every such inference, including implicit links across sentences and unfamiliar wording.

Answer yes only when the writer uses prestige as evidence for a conclusion beyond its relevance. First distinguish the writer's assertion from an embedded assertion. "The document argues X because of someone's status" asserts what the document argues; it does not by itself assert X. Describing that reasoning is a no-hit even when the reasoning is faulty and the writer offers no criticism. A reported recommendation remains the document's recommendation. Neutral dates, prices, arrangements, or event details alone do not establish adoption; an arrangement can express reliance when the passage connects it to the appeal.

Require the writer's own use or adoption of the status-based inference. Documentation, examination, rejection, and a character's limited belief are no-hits unless the passage also advances the inference as its own basis. Reporting an appeal is different from relying on it. Judge the outer writer's reasoning rather than correcting every argument mentioned inside the passage.

For an inference the writer advances, identify the status and the conclusion it supports. Catch unrelated credentials lending weight to advice, fame standing in for reliability, and relevant expertise treated as conclusive proof. The inference can be implied by a recommendation placed beside a status claim; a word such as "therefore" is optional. A writer can adopt a reported appeal by using it to recommend, reassure, or establish the conclusion. Ordinary attribution alone does not exempt that operative claim.

Popularity and recognition of products, services, or organizations are status signals too. Direct recommendations, claims of safety or reliability, and comparative rankings can make the operative inference in a short sentence. An attribution introducing the writer's assertion leaves it eligible; distinguish asserting a product claim with an attributed basis from describing how a document reasons.

Preserve qualifications establishing relevant expertise or eligibility, first-hand experience supporting a bounded observation, and follower counts or media mentions explaining reach or recognition. Relevant advice need not be accompanied by a research paper. Biography, appointments, awards, and credits are no-hits when they describe background rather than establish an unrelated or conclusive claim.

Judge the relevance and strength of the support presented. Exaggerated praise by itself belongs to inflated evaluation; unidentified expert opinion belongs to vague attribution.

## Hits

- "A clinic circular recommends Tomas Vale's sleep programme. **His years as an acclaimed stage director make his medical judgment especially trustworthy.**" The writer supplies the clinical-weight inference outside the circular's reported recommendation.
- "**This climbing harness comes from an award-winning novelist, so you can rely on it to arrest a fall.**" Literary distinction supplies the supposed safety support.
- "Celia's thirty years repairing boats make her useful to consult about hull maintenance. **Her experience also settles whether this ferry can safely carry seventy passengers.** **We can accept her reassurance as its seaworthiness certificate**, omit the inspection, and include the vessel in the summer timetable that goes to our ticket agents." Relevant experience is extended into conclusive verification.
- "The prospectus mentions that the water-filter company's founder plays chess with an Olympic rowing champion. **Buyers can take that friendship as reassurance that the cartridges remove contaminants.**" The writer adopts the irrelevant association as reassurance.

## No-Hits

- "The catalogue argues that the reading course succeeds because its sponsor has won a television award. Access costs eighteen pounds a month; the family subscription covers three accounts." The writer reports the argument and neutral purchase information; the faulty reason belongs to the catalogue.
- "The occupational therapist draws on her work with stroke patients when advising us about these hand exercises." The expertise supports advice within its relevant scope.
- "The exhibition's contributor note lists our lighting designer's theatre awards between her training and her recent commissions. A portrait taken in her first workshop accompanies the note." This describes her background.
- "The council minutes say the footbridge is sound because its designer holds an honorary philosophy chair. The inspection visit is listed for Tuesday morning, after the maintenance meeting." This documents the minutes' rationale.
