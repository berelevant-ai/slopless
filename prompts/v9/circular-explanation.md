# Circular Explanation

Find a claim explained by restating that same claim, including synonyms, paraphrases, or a chain that returns to its starting point. The passage presents an answer to why or how, but the answer adds no explanatory step. Find all instances with unfamiliar wording and across sentences.

## What Counts

- A quality is explained by possessing that quality: reliability comes from being dependable, or an outcome is inevitable because no other result was possible.
- A state or action is explained by another description of the same state or action: indecision comes from being unable to choose.
- A single synonym offered as a reason suffices. A second explicit loop is optional: appropriate because it fits the situation, or evasive because it avoids a direct answer, repeats the assessment.
- An apparent reason can appear in reported speech, a review, a biography, or a document. Attribution alone preserves the circular explanation.
- Names, dates, props, and other unrelated details leave a separate circular explanation intact.

## What Supplies An Explanation

- A distinct cause, observation, rule, operation, constraint, constituent requirement, or dependency adds an explanatory step, even when it repeats vocabulary.
- Check the whole passage. A repeated summary is supported when nearby sentences explain the mechanism behind that same claim.
- A summary of a physical process adds information when surrounding sentences identify what changed and how that produces the result, even if the summary repeats the result's name.
- A whole and its parts can share a name while remaining different: a release requires approval from two offices; the individual approvals constitute the joint decision.
- Recursive definitions and feedback loops are useful when the repeated term refers to another step, part, or application of an explicit rule.
- A bare claim, an acknowledged unknown cause, or repetition alone belongs elsewhere. This check requires a restatement offered as an explanation.
- Preserve a bad explanation quoted for criticism, a lesson, or an editing exercise. Distinguish examining faulty reasoning from reporting it as someone's operative answer.

## Examples

- YES: The service is **dependable because users can depend on it**.
- YES: Why was the ending unavoidable? **No different ending could have happened**.
- YES: Her answer was evasive **because she avoided giving an answer**.
- YES: The setting is suitable **because it fits the intended use**.
- NO: The service is dependable because a second generator takes over when the first fails.
- NO: The room cools faster after we remove the cover blocking its vent. The stronger cooling comes from the air now flowing through the open vent.
- NO: The account stays locked while either of its two separate lock orders is active.
- NO: The tutor uses 'dependable because it can be depended on' to demonstrate an explanation that needs a cause.
- NO: In the editing exercise, students replace 'awkward because it is clumsy' with the obstacle that actually made the task difficult.
