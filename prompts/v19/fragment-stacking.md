# Fragment Stacking

Find clipped sequences that break ordinary explanatory or narrative prose into promotional or dramatic fragment beats. Apply grammar and function, including unfamiliar wording.

- Eligible forms:
  - Two adjacent No/No fragments.
  - Three adjacent ordinary fragments.
  - Two adjacent ordinary fragments immediately followed by a clipped rhetorical payoff.
  - One complete declarative anchor immediately followed by at least two fragments inheriting its subject or predicate.
- An inherited continuation needs the earlier clause to supply its subject or predicate. Complete imperatives have an understood you and remain commands. Complete declarative clauses retain their own subjects even when their verbs are uncommon.
- No/No can be defective with identical or changed wording. A medical, technical or factual subject alone supplies no exemption.
- After checking form and count, judge the sequence's contribution. Preserve genuine structured records, labels, useful lists and explicit short answers when their format serves the visible task. A convenient label or heading alone does not justify dramatic fragmentation.
- Read all connected context; adjacent paragraph breaks can continue the construction. Separate records or unrelated turns establish separate sequences.
- Preserve examined or documentary wording. Assess adopted slogans, quotations and ordinary dialogue normally.

First distinguish separate sentence-like fragments from an ordinary coordinated noun or verb list inside one grammatical sentence. Complete action sentences are not fragments. A continuation may inherit an infinitive predicate as well as a subject; bare verbs after an anchor can therefore continue that anchor rather than address the reader as commands. Preserve genuine UI-stage labels, menu items, reported work entries and direct answers to separately supplied questions when that use is visible. Those uses differ from an ordinary explanation needlessly chopped into beats. A quoted motto adopted by the writer remains eligible.
