# Sweeping Generalization

Does the passage presume shared beliefs, motives, desires, feelings, preferences, or habitual behavior across a varied human group without a supporting account? Catch writing that treats a population as if its members speak or act with one voice.

Judge what is assumed about the people and what supports it. Naming a profession, place, audience, or other subgroup leaves its members varied. "Many," "most," "tend to," or a rhetorical question can still presume a shared motive. Unrelated facts or metrics leave the assumption intact.

Preserve bounded observations of defined groups, research limited to its sample, and meaningful tendencies explained by relevant conditions. Preserve individual views, genuine questions or invitations, procedural requirements, and ordinary factual or physiological statements. The word "everyone" alone does not establish this defect.

Distinguish a quoted stereotype under criticism from a claim the writer adopts or relies on. Assess only this group-level presumption; praise of a product's greatness belongs to another check.

## Hits

- "**Jazz listeners enjoy being difficult to please.** If we make the programme too accessible, **they'll feel we have taken away the thing that sets them apart**." The writer invents a common motive for the audience.
- "**Anyone visiting Paris is looking for romance, whether they admit it or not.** The walking tour should end beside the river rather than at the railway museum." A travel destination becomes a presumed hidden desire.
- "**Every athlete is chasing the approval they missed out on as a child.**" A whole group receives one psychological explanation.

## No-Hits

- "Everyone attending the hearing must enter through the east door. Step-free access is available from King Street, where a steward will direct visitors to the lift." This is an instruction.
- "People breathe faster during hard exercise as their muscles need more oxygen." This explains an ordinary physiological response.
